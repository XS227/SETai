Time Reversal — The Arrow of Time

Time Reversal

SCROLL ↓

Scientific Context
In classical physics, time flows only one way — from past to future. Entropy increases, ice melts, and we age forward.
But quantum mechanics challenges this one-way arrow. Certain experiments suggest that, under specific conditions, the effects of a measurement can appear to influence the past.

In 2019, researchers at the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology (MIPT) used a quantum computer to partially “reverse” the arrow of time, returning a system of qubits to a previous state.
While no physical clock was turned backward, the information in the system was reorganized — as if the quantum wave had remembered its earlier harmony.

“The future and the past are not separate realms, but two mirrors reflecting each other.”
— Y. Ovchinnikov, MIPT Quantum Time Reversal Report, 2019

Similarly, delayed-choice experiments (Wheeler, 1980s) show that decisions made in the present can determine the behavior of particles that, by all classical reasoning, should have already finished their journey.

(Sources: Lesovik et al., Scientific Reports, Nature Publishing Group, 2019; Wheeler, “Law Without Law,” Princeton, 1983; Ma et al., Nature Physics, 2012.)

Philosophical Bridge
If time is not linear, then every moment exists simultaneously — like frames in a cosmic film projected all at once.
What we call “the present” is not a point moving through time, but a window where consciousness selects what to experience.

From this view, destiny and free will are not opposites.
They are quantum partners — the wave and the particle of time itself.

Rumi’s Reflection

“Past and future veil God from our sight;
burn up both of them with fire.”
— Rumi

Rumi invites us to step outside the illusion of before and after.
To him, eternity (azal) is not endless duration but timeless presence — the same stillness at the heart of every moment.
Quantum time reversal shows that the laws of physics may echo this truth:
There is no true separation between cause and effect, between the seed and the flower — they coexist, entangled across time.
