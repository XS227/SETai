The poet

The man who wrote his people back to life

Hakim Abu'l-Qasem Ferdowsi was born around 940 CE in the village of Paj outside Tus, in Khorasan — northeastern Persia. He came from the dehqan class: landowning Persian gentry, seen as carriers of an "authentic," pre-Islamic Persian identity at a time when that identity was under pressure.

He was no cleric, no courtier. A modest, unassuming life — and a task he began at 37 and didn't put down until he was around 70. By then he had written roughly 50,000 verses: the Shahnameh, "the Book of Kings" — likely the longest epic poem ever completed by a single person.

To be honest: no surviving contemporary portrait of Ferdowsi exists. The face you see here — and in the intro film — is our interpretation, built with AI from what the sources actually describe about class, dress, and age. Not a claimed likeness, just an attempt to give a real person a face.

Why he started

A language on the verge of vanishing

When the Arabs conquered Persia in 651 CE, more than a new government arrived — Arabic took over as the language of administration and literature. Over the generations that followed, Persian written culture and pre-Islamic history began to fade.

In the 900s, the Samanid dynasty in Khorasan led a deliberate revival of Persian as a written language. A poet named Daqiqi had begun turning the old Sasanian royal chronicle — the Khwaday-Namag, "Book of Lords" — into verse, but was killed before he got past the Zahhak chapter, around 1,000 lines in.

Ferdowsi picked up where Daqiqi left off, in 977. Not for a court, not for payment that ever arrived as promised — but because someone had to finish it: to give a people their own history back, in their own language, before it was gone for good.

به نام خداوند جان و خرد
"In the name of the Lord of soul and wisdom"
— the opening line. The first of roughly 50,000 verses.

The world's oldest continuous story

From creation to the start of history

The Shahnameh doesn't open with history, but with myth — and slides, era by era, into documented history. Three great ages. One unbroken line.

Pishdadian era
No fixed date — deliberately mythic time
Keyumars — the first man, the first king
No calendar date exists for this era — deliberately so. The text doesn't count in years before any epoch, it counts in reign-lengths, and they're not meant to be historical: Zahhak's own tyranny alone is said to last 1,000 years. That's symbolic mythic time, not a chronology anyone can map onto a BCE date — so we don't invent one. Jamshid's golden age and fall, and Fereydun's fatal division of the world, unfold in this same dateless age.
Kayanian dynasty
Legendary — usually placed before c. 700 BCE
The Age of Heroes
Generations before Cyrus the Great — Manuchehr, Kay Kavus, Kay Khosrow. A heroic age older than the Persian empire most people know from the history books. This is where Rostam and Sohrab belong.
Ashkanian & Sasanian
c. 247 BCE – 651 CE
From legend to documented history
The story slides into Parthian and Sasanian history — well documented, spanning centuries down to the Arab conquest of 651 CE, exactly where Ferdowsi's own project begins.
One man. About 30 years. One unbroken line from the creation of the world to his own lifetime.

The process

How SETAEI built his character with AI

Before anything is generated, we build a character bible: physique, dress, personality — all drawn from actual sources, not invented. It becomes a fixed "Soul Cast" prompt we reuse again and again, so Ferdowsi is recognizable in every scene.

A genuine excerpt from our own production process — not a curated highlight, a working sheet.

We don't settle for a "good enough" first draft

The first generation had a real problem: loose strands of hair broke through the turban in profile, even though the prompt explicitly forbade it. Noted, not hidden — and fixed the next round.

Real film, not a smooth AI face

Our own feedback after the first version: "it still doesn't look like a real human." The next iteration pushes hard toward real skin texture, film grain, and documentary light — practical filmmaking, not a polished render.

Direction, not just image generation

We asked for a specific performance: hesitate, reread, discard, start over — a perfectionist at work, not someone writing fluently. That's direction, not prompting.

Real geography

Paj, at the foot of the mountains outside Tus — a real place, not an invented fantasy landscape. The coordinates are known (36.4767917, 59.6715842); the house shown to tourists there today was actually built around 1920, not Ferdowsi's own — but the landscape, vineyards, and orchards around it are real and undisputed. Sistan, Rostam's homeland, still sits on the map today, split between Iran and Afghanistan.

Four phases, one man

Drag the line. Watch him age.

Instead of a simple before/after: drag the slider through Ferdowsi's four life phases, from the young dehqan in 970 to the exhausted, finished poet in 1020. Same house, same family, one long decline — not a late wealth peak, but a gradual road to poverty, documented in the continuity sheet the production was built on.

Phase 1 · c. 968–978 · age 28–38
The young household
Family: newly married, Mehrak 0–8 years old, Roshanak born toward the end of this phase.
Wealth: comfortable dehqan prosperity — not poor, not rich. The courtyard fountain runs full, the orchard is young.
The man himself: ambitious and curious, still light-hearted. The Shahnameh hasn't started yet — that begins only in 977, when he is 37.
Phase 2 · 979–995 · age 39–55
The household's cultural peak
Family: in 987 — Ferdowsi 47, Mehrnaz 42, Mehrak 17, Roshanak 11. The most alive the household ever gets.
Wealth: the richest and most culturally developed point in the family's life — many guests, many manuscripts, still solid finances.
The man himself: the most productive and driven Ferdowsi. Strong belief in the task, demanding of himself, a warm but often absent father.
the house at its richest
Phase 3 · 996–1008 · age 56–68
The decline begins
Family: Mehrak dies c. 1006–07, age 37 — documented by Ferdowsi himself in his own elegy inside the Shahnameh, not legend.
Wealth: early decay. Lower water in the fountain, overgrowth in the garden, dimmer rooms — same architecture, less upkeep.
The man himself: complained of failing eyesight and leg pain, but kept writing anyway. Grief and self-blame after his son's death color the rest of the work.
Phase 4 · 1009–1020+ · age 69–80+
The finished work
Family: Roshanak the only surviving child, now carrying the household and later the manuscript itself.
Wealth: heavily worn — the driest fountain, the deepest decay of all four phases. No late riches, just a finished work.
The man himself: dignified but exhausted, afraid the text might be lost or destroyed — yet still clear-voiced when reciting it. Roughly 50,000 verses, about 30 years.

Mythology & animals

Not just humans carry this story

Rakhsh
Rostam's horse — the only one that held steady when he pressed his hand against their backs during the selection at Kabul. Ferdowsi describes the coat as "rose petals scattered on a saffron ground." In other tales, Rakhsh fights a lion alone to protect a sleeping Rostam, and senses the trap that kills them both before Rostam himself does. His own Soul ID, his own turnaround — as consistent through the film as Rostam himself.
Simorgh
The mythical bird that rescues and raises Zal — Rostam's father — after he's abandoned as an infant for his white hair. Simorgh later gives Zal one of her feathers, burned to summon her in a crisis. Peacock-eyed tail feathers over a raptor's build — a gap in the mythology collection until this turnaround.

ROSTAM & SOHRAB · PLACE 2

The battlefield

Two armies on a dusty plain. Neither father nor son knows who the other really is.

Two armies meet on a dusty plain. A young warrior rides out alone and calls to the stranger who meets him: are you Rostam, son of Zal? The stranger hesitates a moment too long, and denies it. They fight. Neither knows they are fighting their own blood.

It's one of the most-quoted scenes in Persian literature — and the one we're building first, as our own short film.

Why "the stranger" is unrecognizable: Rostam rides out hiding his identity — armor and the tiger-pelt cloak both covered by a plain, dirty soldier's cloak, no banner. See his gallery above for the reference: full armor for the fight itself, this disguise for the ride out and the "I am no one you know" beat, before the cloak comes off at the recognition.

✦ How this actually gets built

The pipeline, not just the pitch

Higgsfield's own open-sourced films — Hell Grind, Zephyr, Mork — ship with the reasoning behind each technique, not just the finished cut. Here's ours, in the same spirit: what actually keeps a character's face, a location's light, and a prop's identity consistent across dozens of separate AI generations.

Locked reference sheets, not re-described characters
Every character (Rostam, Sohrab, Tahmineh, Ferdowsi across four life phases) has a fixed turnaround/pose sheet generated once and referenced by every later shot — never re-described from scratch. This is the single biggest lever against the "AI face drift" a jury notices instantly.
One through-line prop, tracked across every appearance
The armband exists as its own isolated reference sheet (clasp, stone, worn-on-arm scale) and appears identically in the gifting scene, the departure scene, and the death scene — the recognition has to work as an actual visual match, not just as a line of dialogue.
Location sheets before character sheets enter them
Samangan, the two war camps, and the battlefield itself are generated as clean environment plates first — then characters are placed into them, and the same plate is deliberately reused across shots (e.g. the wide battlefield shot opens and closes the duel) instead of regenerating similar-but-different terrain each time.
A shared avoid-list, not per-shot guesswork
Every shot's prompt carries the same negative-prompt discipline — no glowing/magical effects, no oversized moon, no readable on-screen text, no loose hair breaking Ferdowsi's turban. Collected once on the camera page so it's a system, not a memory test.
Fact-tiered research before a single prompt is written
Every character/location claim is tagged against what Ferdowsi's text actually says, what's later artistic tradition, and what's this production's own creative choice — so the film can say honestly which parts are Shahnameh and which parts are us.
Budget re-generation by emotional weight, not by shot count
The recognition, the dagger strike, and the closing pull-back are marked "never cut, spend the most re-generation budget here" — a jury forgives an imperfect crowd shot far faster than it forgives a hollow face in the one moment the whole film is built around.
None of this is claimed to match Hell Grind's own 95-minute, crowd-scale pipeline exactly — it's the same underlying discipline (locked references, tracked props, shared constraints) scaled to a 4:23 short. Full detail: manuscript · camera notes.
